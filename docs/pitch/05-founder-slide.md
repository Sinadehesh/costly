# The founder slide

## Should Guardbar go in? Yes, with a hard limit on what you claim.

**Include it.** Founding a company that earned a place at Shiraz Science and
Technology Park is a fact about your life, and it is the direct answer to a
question Antler is known to ask: *have you taken on sustained risk?* Missing
paperwork does not make it untrue, and nobody at pre-seed asks for share
certificates. Most early startups leave no trace on the internet; that is
normal, not suspicious.

**What you may say, because none of it needs a document:**

> Co-founded Guardbar. Accepted into Shiraz Science and Technology Park.
> Left the company when I emigrated.

**What you must not say:**

- **"Exited"** or **"sold my shares."** The sale never completed. Your
  co-founder did not honour the handshake, so a payout that did not happen must
  not be implied. "Left when I emigrated" is true and costs you nothing.
- **Any metric.** No users, revenue, funding, or valuation. The moment you
  attach a number you cannot evidence, an unverifiable line becomes a
  falsifiable one.
- **Do not lead with it.** It is a supporting fact, not the headline. The
  headline is what you can do now.

**If a partner asks what happened,** tell them plainly: you agreed a buyout on a
handshake, emigrated, and it was never honoured. That answer helps you. It is a
real lesson about why founder agreements get papered, and a partner would rather
hear it in one calm sentence than discover you were vague about it. Do not
volunteer it; do not dodge it.

## The slide

**Do not make this a CV.** Everyone's founder slide lists jobs. Yours has one
claim nobody else in this category can make, so the slide exists to land that
claim and then get out of the way.

### Headline

> # I can build it, and I can prove it works.

### Subhead

> Sina Dehesh. MSc Applied Experimental Psychological Sciences,
> University of Milano-Bicocca.

### One line under the subhead: how you work

> I build products out of the literature, not out of competitors.
> Costly's model is a deposit contract, straight from the behavioural research.

### Three blocks, one line of evidence each

**THE SCIENTIST**
Thesis: within-subjects, **N=96**, three conditions, RM-ANOVA,
Holm-corrected, **p=.009**.
200 clinical interviews on compulsive behaviour.

**THE BUILDER**
**20+ products and prototypes in the last year.** The ones that survived
contact with users: OopsCupid (live), two Pipedrive products (3 and 5 months),
Engineer Passway.
Costly is solo: full Stripe hold-and-capture lifecycle plus a Play-compliant
Android detection engine.

**THE FOUNDER**
Co-founded **Guardbar**; accepted into Shiraz Science and Technology Park.
Nine cold user interviews for Costly, and six product changes shipped from
what they said.

### Closing line, in the accent colour

> Every competitor in this category makes a correlational claim.
> I can run the trial.

## Why this shape

**The closing line is the whole slide.** Digital wellbeing is full of apps
claiming usage went down among people who use them, which is correlation and
everybody in the room knows it. You have run a within-subjects study with a
significant result on a harder outcome. That is not a nice-to-have credential;
it is the one thing on the slide a competitor cannot copy by hiring.

**"Nine cold user interviews, six changes shipped" beats the degree** for a
day-zero programme. The degree says you were trained to do research. That line
says you did it last week, on this product, and acted on it. Antler is reading
the founder, not the CV.

**Guardbar sits third on purpose.** It answers "has this person carried risk
before" without inviting questions you would rather answer in conversation than
on a slide.

**What is deliberately absent:** certifications, the core-competencies list,
anything from the CV that reads as a job application. A founder slide that looks
like a résumé signals someone applying for a role rather than starting a
company.

## The two additions that needed reframing

### "20+ applications in the last year"

Powerful, and the single most double-edged line on the slide. Said alone it
invites the obvious rebuttal: *twenty in a year means none of them are real, and
this person does not finish things.* Volume reads as dabbling, and dabbling is
the opposite of what a programme selecting for obsessive focus wants to see.

Said with the survivors attached, it flips into exactly the trait they are
screening for: **build, test, kill, repeat, fast.** So the number never appears
on its own. It is always followed by "the ones that survived contact with
users", and then real products with real timelines. Twenty attempts and four
that stuck is a functioning process. Twenty attempts alone is a graveyard.

Phrased as "products and prototypes" on purpose. If some of the twenty were
weekend experiments, "20 applications" overstates it, and the first partner who
asks to see them all will find that out.

### "I read finance and business books and apply them"

As stated, this is the weakest thing you could put on the slide. Everybody says
it, it describes an input rather than an output, and a partner hears it as
filler. It also competes with the strongest claim on the slide, which is a
serious problem on a slide with one job.

But the real version of it is genuinely one of your best arguments, because you
have already done the thing: **Costly's business model is a deposit contract
lifted from the behavioural literature, not copied from a competitor.** The 20/80
split, loss aversion, self-chosen stakes, the Beeminder precedent, the caveat
that deposits must be small and self-selected. That is not a reading habit, it
is a product built out of research.

So it goes on the slide as a claim about how you build, immediately backed by
the product in the room:

> I build products out of the literature, not out of competitors.
> Costly's model is a deposit contract, straight from the behavioural research.

If asked to expand, name the specific finding: deposit contracts beat reward
schemes because loss aversion is stronger than gain, and the literature's own
caveat, that stakes must be small and self-chosen, is why the user sets their
own rate rather than being assigned one.

## Speaker notes, roughly 30 seconds

> I am a psychologist who ships. My thesis was a within-subjects study, ninety-
> six participants, on whether feedback changes behaviour, and it found a
> significant effect. I have also run two hundred clinical interviews on
> compulsive behaviour, so this is not a topic I picked up recently.
>
> And I build a lot. Twenty-odd products and prototypes in the last year; four
> survived contact with users, and two of those were client work delivered on
> fixed timelines. Costly is the full payment system and the Android detection
> engine, written solo.
>
> I build them out of the research rather than by copying competitors. Costly's
> model is a deposit contract, which is straight from the behavioural
> literature: loss moves people harder than gain, and the same research says the
> stake has to be small and self-chosen, which is why the user sets their own
> rate.
>
> The reason that combination matters: every company in this space claims usage
> went down among their users. That is correlation. I can run the randomised
> trial, and I would rather have an effect size than a testimonial.
